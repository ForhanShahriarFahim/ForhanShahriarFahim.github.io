Research

I want machine learning systems to be trustworthy in the specific, unglamorous sense that matters to the people who use them: a practitioner should be able to see why a model made a decision, and the model should not require an impossible amount of labelled data to be built in the first place.

Overview

Two obstacles keep deep models out of high-stakes practice. The first is opacity: a radiologist or a security engineer cannot act on a prediction they cannot interrogate. The second is annotation cost: the domains where these models would help most are exactly the ones where expert labels are scarce and expensive. My work approaches these as one problem rather than two, because a model that learns good representations from unlabelled data is also, usually, a model whose representations are worth explaining.

So far I have worked on the interpretability side in software security, where large labelled corpora exist and explanation quality can be evaluated against known vulnerability classes. I am now moving the same methods into medical imaging, where the label scarcity problem is severe and the need for explanation is acute.

Research Threads

1. Interpretability and trustworthy machine learning

My published work studies whether transformer models can be made to explain their own decisions well enough to be acted upon. In a framework published in the Journal of Cyber Security Technology, we trained a multi-task transformer that detects a source-code vulnerability while simultaneously predicting its CWE category and source language. The auxiliary tasks are the interesting part: forcing the model to name the weakness class alongside the binary decision yields a prediction an engineer can triage, not just a score.

In related work we ran an empirical analysis of LIME attributions over transformer predictions for JavaScript vulnerabilities, asking how stable and how faithful those local explanations actually are, and a hybrid static–dynamic analysis of Android applications that categorises discovered vulnerabilities by CWE and pairs them with concrete mitigations.

The open question I find most interesting is whether post-hoc attributions like LIME are the right tool at all, or whether interpretability has to be designed into the architecture and the training objective from the start.

2. Computer vision

I am currently benchmarking YOLO architectures (v8 through v12) for instance segmentation of crop leaf diseases, comparing the Nano, Small, and Medium variants to characterise the trade-off between segmentation accuracy and computational cost. For this I built a custom dataset with pixel-level polygon annotations, since existing crop-disease datasets are almost entirely classification-only.

My undergraduate thesis addressed a related robustness question in a different modality: a hybrid CNN–LSTM framework for force-invariant surface EMG pattern recognition, which reached 80% classification accuracy across varying muscle contraction forces and outperformed ResNet-50 and VGG-16 baselines under the same shift.

3. Health and medical AI

I lead a PRISMA-guided systematic review of self-supervised learning methods for label-efficient medical image analysis, covering MRI, CT, and histopathology. The review synthesises pretext-task designs, benchmark datasets, and evaluation protocols, with the aim of identifying which self-supervised objectives actually transfer to clinical tasks rather than merely to ImageNet-style linear probes.

I also supervise undergraduate research in this area, including work on brain tumour segmentation using attention mechanisms and on automated tumour detection.
