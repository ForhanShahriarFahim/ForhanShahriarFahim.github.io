Research Interests

I want machine learning systems to be trustworthy in the specific, unglamorous sense that matters to the people who use them: a practitioner should be able to see why a model made a decision, and the model should not require an impossible amount of labelled data to be built in the first place.

Overview

Two obstacles keep deep models out of high-stakes practice. The first is opacity: a radiologist or a security engineer cannot act on a prediction they cannot interrogate. The second is annotation cost: the domains where these models would help most are exactly the ones where expert labels are scarce and expensive. My work approaches these as one problem rather than two, because a model that learns good representations from unlabelled data is also, usually, a model whose representations are worth explaining.

So far I have worked on the interpretability side in software security, where large labelled corpora exist and explanation quality can be evaluated against known vulnerability classes. I completed a review of self-supervised learning in cancer imaging in September 2026. I am interested in applying the same questions about evidence and reliability to medical language models, especially uncertainty and abstention, and to vision-language models that combine image and text evidence.

Focus Areas

1. AI safety and interpretability

My published work studies whether transformer models can be made to explain their own decisions well enough to be acted upon. In a framework published in the Journal of Cyber Security Technology, we trained a multi-task transformer that detects a source-code vulnerability while simultaneously predicting its CWE category and source language. The auxiliary tasks are the interesting part: forcing the model to name the weakness class alongside the binary decision yields a prediction an engineer can triage, not just a score.

In related work we ran an empirical analysis of LIME attributions over transformer predictions for JavaScript vulnerabilities, asking how stable and how faithful those local explanations actually are, and a hybrid static–dynamic analysis of Android applications that categorises discovered vulnerabilities by CWE and pairs them with concrete mitigations.

Our accepted RAAICON 2026 paper studies dual-encoder cross-attention for multi-language vulnerability patch generation. It extends this software-security work from identifying flaws to generating candidate repairs.

The open question I find most interesting is whether post-hoc attributions like LIME are the right tool at all, or whether interpretability has to be designed into the architecture and the training objective from the start.

2. Large language models

I am interested in the reliability of large language models, particularly how uncertainty and abstention can be evaluated in medical settings. I hope to study these questions in doctoral research.

3. Computer vision

I am interested in robust visual representations and segmentation for medical imaging. I supervise undergraduate projects on brain tumour segmentation using attention mechanisms and on automated tumour detection.

My undergraduate thesis addressed model robustness in a different modality: a hybrid CNN–LSTM framework for force-invariant surface EMG pattern recognition, which reached 80% classification accuracy across varying muscle contraction forces. I benchmarked it against ResNet-50 and VGG-16.

4. Vision-language models

I am interested in how visual evidence and language interact in medical AI, and how to assess the reliability of systems that combine them. This is a future research direction. My completed cancer-imaging review, covering MRI, CT, and histopathology, informs my interest in the quality and limits of medical-image evidence. Its manuscript is in preparation.

Earlier Work

Force-Invariant EMG Pattern Recognition

Apr 2023 – Sep 2024

Undergraduate thesis · Supervisor: Professor Shamim Ahmad, University of Rajshahi

  • Developed a hybrid CNN–LSTM framework for force-invariant surface EMG pattern recognition.
  • Benchmarked against ResNet-50 and VGG-16 under varying contraction forces.
  • Achieved 80% classification accuracy across dynamic contraction levels, demonstrating robustness to variation in muscle contraction force.
