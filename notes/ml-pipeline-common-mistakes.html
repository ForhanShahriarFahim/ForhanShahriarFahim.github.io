The Machine Learning Pipeline, and Where It Usually Breaks

28 August 2026 · 9 min read

Most machine learning projects that fail do not fail at the model. They fail somewhere in the plumbing around it — in how the data was split, how a metric was chosen, how a preprocessing step was fitted. The architecture is the part everyone discusses and the part that least often decides the outcome.

What follows is the pipeline end to end, with the mistake I most often see at each stage. I am not writing from a position of never having made them: most of these I have made myself, and the rest I now watch for when I supervise undergraduate theses. The pattern is consistent enough to be worth writing down.

The stages

  1. Frame the problem before touching data

    Decide what decision the model informs and what a wrong answer costs. Those costs are almost never symmetric: a missed tumour and a false alarm are both errors, but they are not the same error, and the metric you optimise should say so.

    Common mistake: choosing a metric because it is conventional rather than because it reflects the cost of being wrong. Accuracy on a dataset that is 95% negative is a constant predictor wearing a disguise — it will score 95% while detecting nothing.

  2. Look at the data before modelling it

    Plot the distributions. Count the classes. Look for duplicates, for impossible values, for a suspiciously predictive column. Open a random sample and actually read it.

    Common mistake: trusting the labels. Label noise is normal, particularly where annotation is expert-dependent and expensive — which describes most medical datasets. If you can measure inter-annotator agreement, measure it. If you cannot, hand-inspect a random sample and find out empirically how clean your ceiling is.

  3. Split the data, and split it first

    The split should happen before any transformation, any imputation, any balancing. Everything downstream is defined relative to it.

    Common mistake: splitting randomly when the records are grouped. Several slices from one patient, several functions from one source file, several frames from one video — a random split puts the same underlying entity on both sides, and your test score becomes a memorisation score. Split by group. For anything time-ordered, split by time, and never train on the future.

  4. Fit preprocessing on the training set only

    Scaling, imputation, encoding, vocabulary construction, feature selection, dimensionality reduction — each of these learns something from data, and each must learn it from training data alone.

    Common mistake: computing a scaler, a mean, or a feature ranking over the whole dataset and splitting afterwards. The same applies to oversampling: running SMOTE before the split copies synthetic neighbours of test points into the training set. Wrap the steps in a pipeline object so the wrong order becomes structurally impossible rather than merely discouraged.

  5. Establish a baseline you would be embarrassed to lose to

    A majority-class predictor. A logistic regression on a handful of features. A small gradient-boosted tree. These take an afternoon.

    Common mistake: skipping them entirely. Without a baseline you cannot tell whether the transformer earned its complexity, and the honest answer is often that the gap is far narrower than expected. That is worth discovering in week one rather than month three.

  6. Train reproducibly

    Fix the seeds. Log the configuration alongside the result. Checkpoint, so that the number in your notes can be traced to the weights that produced it.

    Common mistake: tuning against the test set. Every time you read test performance and change something in response, you move information from the test set into your model — slowly, and without any single moment that feels like cheating. Make decisions on a validation split. Touch the test set once, at the end.

  7. Evaluate with uncertainty attached

    Report the metric that matches the framing you chose in stage one, and report it with some indication of how much it moves.

    Common mistake: a single number from a single split. Run several seeds and report the spread. A two-point improvement is not an improvement if seed variance is three points, and this is probably the most common reason published gains fail to reproduce.

  8. Interpret carefully

    Attribution methods — LIME, SHAP, Grad-CAM and their relatives — tell you what the model responded to. They do not tell you why the underlying phenomenon is true, and they are approximations with their own hyperparameters.

    Common mistake: treating an attribution as ground truth. Run the same explainer twice with different perturbation samples and the ranking can shift. Check that an explanation is stable before you build an argument on top of it. This is close to my own research, and the honest summary is that faithfulness is still an open problem rather than a solved one.

  9. Assume the world will move

    A deployed model sits in a world that keeps changing: scanners get replaced, coding conventions evolve, users adapt to the system's behaviour.

    Common mistake: assuming the distribution holds. A model that is not monitored is not a model whose accuracy you know — it is a model whose accuracy you knew, once.

Leakage deserves its own warning

The most expensive mistake

Of everything above, data leakage is the one that costs the most, because it does not announce itself. It produces better numbers, which is exactly the direction that stops people from investigating.

A rule that catches most cases: if a piece of information would not be available at the moment the model is asked to make a real prediction, it must not be available at training time either. Grouped records, temporal ordering, and preprocessing fitted across the split are the three usual sources.

The structural defence is to make the correct order the path of least resistance. Rather than scaling a matrix and then splitting it, put the transformation inside the estimator so it is re-fitted within every fold: 

from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import GroupKFold, cross_val_score

pipe = Pipeline([
    ("scale", StandardScaler()),
    ("clf",   LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)),
])

# The scaler is re-fitted inside each fold, on that fold's training data only,
# and the groups keep every patient entirely on one side of the split.
scores = cross_val_score(
    pipe, X, y,
    cv=GroupKFold(n_splits=5),
    groups=patient_ids,
    scoring="average_precision",
)
print(f"{scores.mean():.3f} +/- {scores.std():.3f}")

Two details in that snippet matter more than the pipeline itself. groups=patient_ids is what prevents the same patient appearing on both sides of a fold, and average_precision is chosen over accuracy because it degrades honestly under class imbalance. Printing the standard deviation alongside the mean is the whole of stage seven.

A checklist

This is what I actually run through, roughly in order:

  • Can I state the decision this model informs, and the cost of each kind of error?
  • Have I looked at raw examples, including a sample of the labels?
  • Is the split grouped by the right entity, and ordered by time where time matters?
  • Is every fitted transformation inside the pipeline rather than applied beforehand?
  • Do I have a trivial baseline, and do I know the gap to it?
  • Are seeds fixed and configurations logged next to results?
  • Have I reported variation across seeds, not a single number?
  • Have I checked that my explanations are stable before believing them?
  • Do I know what I would monitor if this ran in production tomorrow?

None of this is where the interesting research happens, and that is rather the point. The unglamorous stages are where results are actually made or quietly invalidated, and they are cheap to get right if you decide to get them right before you start rather than during the rebuttal.
